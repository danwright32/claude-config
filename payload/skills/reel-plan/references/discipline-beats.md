# Discipline beat sheets

Read only the section that matches the event.

Each section answers four questions. Where does the story live? What is the shot you must not miss? What is the peak, and when does it fall? What goes wrong here specifically?

A note that applies to all of them: **the highest engagement material in performing arts video is usually not the polished performance.** Rehearsal, backstage, personality, and the faces of the people watching consistently outperform a clean record of the stage. Most arts organizations already have someone recording the show. Almost none of them have anyone shooting the twenty minutes before it.

---

## Choir

**Where the story lives.** In the collective, and then in one face. A choir is a single organism that resolves into individuals. The reel earns its ending when the wide of ninety people becomes a tight shot of one of them.

**Must not miss.** The conductor's downbeat on the first entrance. The intake of breath before it. The moment the last chord releases and the hall keeps ringing.

**Peak.** Usually the loudest, highest passage of the final piece, but not always. Listen in advance. Sometimes the peak is a pianissimo.

**What goes wrong.** Rows of faces at the same size and angle, which cannot be cut together. Vary the size deliberately. Also: choirs stand still, so the frame must move or the reel dies. Get hands, sheet music, the conductor from behind, breath.

---

## Orchestra and chamber ensemble

**Where the story lives.** In hands and in transactions. A bow lifting. A page turn. Two players looking at each other and arriving together.

**Must not miss.** The concertmaster's tuning A. The conductor's hands. The soloist's first entrance. The bow lifting off the string on the final note, held, before the applause breaks.

**Peak.** The last thirty seconds of the last movement, and then the silence before applause. Do not stop rolling at the last note. The silence is the shot.

**What goes wrong.** Shooting only the conductor's face, which the audience never sees. Shooting long lens handheld. Forgetting that the second violins exist and the client's donor plays second violin.

---

## Opera

**Where the story lives.** In faces at maximum emotional extremity, and in scale. Opera is the only discipline where the wide shot and the extreme closeup are both indispensable.

**Must not miss.** The aria's final held note. The set at full stage with the orchestra pit visible. A hand on a costume.

**Peak.** The end of the big aria, and the curtain call, which is often the best footage of the night because the artifice drops.

**What goes wrong.** Scale defeats the tight shot. From a house seat with a 70-200 you will not reach a face on a large stage, and the 8K crop off the locked wide is where your closeups actually come from. Plan the wide's framing with that in mind, tighter than instinct says.

---

## Play, spoken theatre

**Where the story lives.** In the exchange between two actors, and in the audience's stillness.

**Must not miss.** A clean two shot of the central relationship. The moment before the biggest line, not just the line. The set, empty, lit, before anyone walks on. That empty stage is almost always the cold open.

**Peak.** The climax of the plot, which you can find in advance by reading the script or asking the director one question: where does it turn?

**What goes wrong.** Two things, and both are fatal. **The rights,** because a licensed play usually forbids recording outright. And **the dialogue,** because a camera microphone in a reverberant house will not give you an intelligible line. Pick two or three lines that must be heard and solve those specifically.

**The access you should ask for.** Professional theatre companies routinely hold a final dress rehearsal designated as a photo call: full costume, full tech, no audience. Photographers shoot it in preference to the performance. It is standard, it costs the company nothing, and asking for it is the single highest leverage thing a photographer new to theatre can do. Shoot the dress for the stage, and the performance for the room.

---

## Musical theatre

**Hybrid grammar.** Get the running order and mark each block: song, dialogue, dance. Each block obeys its own grammar. Applying one grammar to the whole piece produces a reel that either loses every joke or has no lift.

**Where the story lives.** In the transition from speech into song. That moment, where a person stops talking and starts singing, is what the form is for, and it is the shot the reel is built around.

**Must not miss.** The first bar of the opening number with the full company on stage. The button at the end of a big number, arms up, held, before the applause. One face during a quiet song.

**Peak.** The eleven o'clock number, or in a short piece, the last song.

**What goes wrong.** Grand rights. If the musical is licensed from a rights house, the license to perform it almost never includes the right to record it, and the company cannot grant you permission it does not itself have. Establish that the work is original, or that recording rights were separately obtained, before you take the job. This is the most common way a musical theatre reel dies.

## Comedy

**Where the story lives.** In the room, not on the stage. A joke without a laugh is a person talking.

**Must not miss.** The laugh, and specifically the audience during the laugh. Faces mid laugh, someone hitting a friend's arm, a person doubled over. The performer waiting out the laugh, which is the shot that proves it landed.

**Peak.** The biggest laugh of the set. You cannot know it in advance, which means you must be rolling continuously and shooting far more than you use.

**What goes wrong.** Cutting the laugh short to save time. Never. The laugh is the payoff and it must be allowed to run. Also: intelligible dialogue is the whole game here, so proximity matters more than composition.

---

## Dance and movement work

**Where the story lives.** In whole bodies in space, and in the accent, the instant a body arrives at a shape.

**Must not miss.** A wide that holds the full body, feet included. Cutting off a dancer's feet is a crime and every dancer in the room will see it. Then the unison moment, when a line of dancers snaps together.

**Peak.** The final tableau, and the lift or jump immediately before it.

**Slow motion earns its keep here**, at 60 frames per second, on the accents, with sound covered by music. But test the frame rate against the stage lights first, because dance is lit with LED fixtures and slow motion is where they band.

**What goes wrong.** Rights, again, and worse. Dance is usually set to recorded commercial music played over the house speakers. Your camera captures it, the platform hears it, the reel is muted. Establish this before you shoot, because if the audio is unpostable you have lost sync entirely and need far more coverage and far more slow motion than you planned.

---

## Band, amplified music

**Where the story lives.** In the crowd as much as the stage, and in one player's hands.

**Must not miss.** The drummer, who is always shot from too far away. Hands on a fretboard. The singer's face at the top of a chorus. The crowd from the stage side.

**Peak.** The final chorus of the biggest song.

**What goes wrong.** The audio, which is loud enough to distort a camera microphone badly. Ask for a board feed. Also, the light changes every four seconds and your locked exposure will be wrong for half of it. Expose for the brightest state, not the average.

---

## Children's programming, family shows

**Where the story lives.** Not on the stage. On the faces in the first three rows.

A show for very young children is an experiment in whether four adults can convince a room of toddlers of something impossible. The reel is the record of that experiment succeeding, and the evidence is on the children, not the performers. Shoot the stage for coverage, and shoot the room for the story.

**Must not miss.** A child's face at the moment of belief. A child copying a gesture from the stage. A parent watching their child rather than the show.

**Peak.** Whenever the room breaks: laughing, shouting back, standing up.

**What goes wrong.** Consent. Identifiable children in footage you use to market your own business is commercial use of a minor's likeness, and it requires a guardian's release. Plan for it: shoot the audience from behind, hands, silhouettes, backs of heads with the stage sharp beyond them, a wide where no face reads. Give the client the version with the faces. Keep the unidentifiable version for yourself.
