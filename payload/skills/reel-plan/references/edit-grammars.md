# The four edit grammars

The grammar answers one question: **what does a cut land on?** Everything about the shot list follows from that answer, because a cut you cannot land is a cut you cannot make, no matter how good the footage is.

Pick one. Hybrid is a real answer, not a hedge.

---

## Driven by music

Concert, recital, orchestra, choir, opera, chamber ensemble.

**The cut lands on** a musical event: a downbeat, an entrance, a key change, the silence after a phrase, the last chord.

**The hard part.** The music is chosen by the program, not by you. You cannot pick a track with a convenient tempo, and you cannot cut away from a musical phrase in the middle without the audience hearing the seam. The audio is continuous and unforgiving.

**What that demands of the shot list.** Multiple angles of the same passage, or you can never cut at all. In pinned mode this is what the locked off wide is for: it holds while you cut in and out of tight shots from the second body. In roaming mode, where there is no continuous wide, you are limited to using each passage exactly once, so you must plan which passage each angle belongs to before the concert starts.

**The advance move.** If you know the repertoire, listen to it beforehand. Mark where the peak is, where the quiet is, where the piece resolves. That is your paper edit, handed to you by a composer.

**Where reels fail.** Cutting on a visual impulse instead of a musical one. The music tells you when to cut. Obey it.

---

## Driven by speech

Play, comedy, spoken theatre, keynote, panel.

**The cut lands on** the end of a line, a reaction, or a laugh.

**The structure.** A licensed music bed runs underneath, and it is punctured by pockets of clean sync sound where a line or a laugh lands. The bed carries the montage. The sync pockets carry the meaning. Three or four pockets in a two minute piece.

**The hard part.** Intelligible dialogue from a house seat, in a reverberant room, on a camera microphone, is genuinely difficult, harder than music. Music survives reverb. Consonants do not. Decide before the shoot which two or three lines must be heard, and solve those specifically: get close, get the feed, or cut the line.

**The gift.** You choose the music. Because the event supplies no score, you can select the bed weeks in advance, know its tempo, know where it swells, and design the entire cut against it before you shoot a frame. This is the *easier* planning problem, not the harder one.

**Comedy is a special case.** The laugh is the beat. The audience is half your audio and half your coverage. A joke without the laugh is a person talking. Get the room's sound, and get faces reacting. The reel is a record of the room working.

---

## Driven by movement

Dance, physical theatre, circus, movement based work.

**The cut lands on** a physical accent: the peak of a jump, a landing, a body hitting a shape, a line of dancers snapping into unison.

**What that demands.** Wide shots that hold whole bodies, because cutting off a dancer's feet is a crime and everyone in the room will notice. Then tight shots for texture, never for the movement itself. Slow motion earns its keep here, at 60 frames per second, on the accents, with the sound covered by music.

**The rights problem.** Movement work is usually set to recorded playback over the house speakers, and that playback is frequently a commercial track. Camera audio captures it, the platform's content matching hears it, and the reel is muted or blocked after you have already edited it. Settle this before you shoot. If the audio is unpostable, you are shooting for a silent cut with a library bed, which changes the shot list: you lose sync entirely, so you need far more coverage and far more slow motion.

---

## Hybrid

Musical theatre, opera with spoken dialogue, a concert with introductions, a comedy set with songs.

**The cut lands on** whichever grammar owns the current beat, and the reel moves between them.

**The structure.** Treat the piece as a sequence of blocks. This block is a song, so it obeys the music grammar. This block is dialogue, so it obeys the speech grammar. This block is a chase across the stage, so it obeys movement.

**The advance move.** Get the song list or the running order and mark each block's grammar before the shoot. Then the shot list writes itself, block by block, and you know when to be greedy for coverage and when to just hold the wide and let a scene play.

**Where reels fail.** Applying one grammar to the whole piece. A musical cut entirely to music loses every joke. A musical cut entirely to dialogue has no lift.

---

## Choosing the bed when you get to choose

When the grammar is speech or the audio is unpostable, you pick the music. Rules:

- Pick it before the shoot, not after. The whole point is to plan against it.
- Instrumental. Lyrics fight dialogue and they fight the event.
- A track with an obvious build. You need something to rise into at the peak of the paper edit.
- Cleared for commercial use, from a subscription library. The reel is going on your site and eventually into a paid package.
- Match the event's dignity. A children's musical and a requiem are not scored the same way, and neither is scored like a car advertisement.
