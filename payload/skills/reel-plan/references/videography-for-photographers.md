# Videography for a photographer

Everything you already know about light, composition, and anticipation transfers. What follows is only the part that does not.

## 1. A shot is not a frame

A photograph is an instant. A video shot is a **duration**, and durations have a minimum. A shot that can survive an edit runs six to ten seconds of steady, held framing.

Roll three seconds before the thing happens and keep rolling three seconds after it ends. You throw those ends away in the edit. Without them you have no handles, and a cut with no handles cannot be trimmed, dissolved, or timed to music.

The most common beginner failure is not bad shots. It is shots that are two seconds long.

## 2. Do not reframe while rolling

Zooming, panning, or hunting for composition mid shot destroys the shot. There is no crop in post that fixes a wobble.

If the framing is wrong: **stop recording, reframe, roll again.** Two clean shots always beat one shot with a scramble in the middle.

A camera move is allowed only when it is deliberate, slow, started before the action and finished after it, and committed to for the entire duration. Otherwise, hold still and let the performers move through your frame. Motion inside a static frame is almost always better than motion of the frame.

## 3. Shutter speed is no longer yours

This is the hardest habit to break. In stills, shutter speed is an exposure control. In video it is a **look** control, and it is nearly fixed.

The rule of thumb, sometimes called the 180 degree shutter: **set shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate.**

- 24 frames per second, shutter 1/50
- 30 frames per second, shutter 1/60
- 60 frames per second, shutter 1/125

Shoot a performance at 1/500 because that is what you would do for stills and the footage will look stuttery and cheap, like security camera video. Motion blur is what makes motion read as motion.

Since shutter is spoken for, exposure is controlled with **aperture and ISO**, and outdoors with a neutral density filter. On a lit stage, open the aperture and raise the ISO, expose for the face, and let the background fall away. Stage lighting is contrasty. Protect the highlights on skin.

## 4. Frame rate is a decision about time

- **24 frames per second.** The default. Looks like film. Sound plays at normal speed.
- **60 frames per second.** Shoot this only when you intend to slow the shot down. Conformed to a 24 frame timeline it plays at 40 percent speed, which is beautiful for a gesture, a bow lifting, a child's laugh beginning.

The trap: **slowed footage has no usable sound.** Never shoot a line of dialogue, a punchline, or a musical passage you need to hear at 60 frames per second. Slow motion is for moments you will cover with music.

The second trap is in the next section, and it is the reason slow motion under stage lights often comes back ruined.

## 4b. LED stage lighting will band your footage

Modern stage fixtures and LED video walls dim by switching on and off very fast, a technique called PWM. Your eye reads it as steady light. Your sensor reads it as flicker, and it shows up as rolling horizontal bands across the frame, or as brightness pulsing between frames. It is not fixable in post.

Three things protect you.

- **Use a slower shutter.** A slow shutter averages across many switching cycles and smooths the banding out. This is one more reason to hold the 180 degree rule instead of reaching for a fast shutter.
- **Be suspicious of slow motion under LED wash.** Typical stage and rental LED fixtures switch somewhere around 2,000 to 4,000 times a second, which is barely enough at normal frame rates and frequently not enough at 60 frames per second or above. Dimmed fixtures are worse, because the switching pulses get very short. If you plan a slow motion shot, plan it under tungsten, daylight, or a fixture you have tested.
- **Use the camera's flicker tools.** The Z8 has high frequency flicker reduction with shutter fine tuning and selectable frequency presets. See `references/gear-nikon-z8.md`, including the honest caveat about whether the fine tuning is offered while recording video.
- **Test before the house opens.** Point the camera at the lit stage during setup, at the frame rate and shutter you intend to use, and look at the back of the camera. Thirty seconds of testing beats discovering it in the edit.

If you see banding and cannot change the light, change the shutter first, then the frame rate. Do not change the aperture and hope.

## 5. Lock everything automatic

Auto white balance drifts mid shot and cannot be repaired. Auto ISO pumps the exposure up and down as a performer crosses a spotlight. Autofocus hunts, and a focus hunt in the middle of the best moment of the night is a shot in the bin.

Lock white balance. Lock ISO. If your camera's face detection is genuinely reliable, use it. Otherwise pull focus manually and stop down a little further than instinct says, because a video shot that drifts out of focus for two seconds is dead, where a soft photograph is merely a soft photograph.

## 6. You cannot cut two similar shots together

Cut from a wide of the stage to a slightly wider wide of the stage and the image jumps. The viewer sees the edit. This is a **jump cut** and it is the single most common reason footage turns out to be uncuttable.

To cut cleanly between two shots of the same subject, change **either the size or the angle, meaningfully.** Roughly thirty degrees of angle, or a full step in framing (wide to medium, medium to tight). Anything less reads as a mistake.

This has a consequence that governs the entire shot list. **For any moment you want to appear in the reel, you need either two cameras on it, or two genuinely different framings of it captured at different times.** A single continuous performance shot from a single position gives you exactly one usable piece of that moment, and it must run uncut for as long as you want to use it.

This is why a locked off camera, rolling untouched, is worth more than any other shot you can get. It is the angle you can always cut back to. It is also why the parked window exists, and why choosing where to spend it decides what the reel can be.

## 7. Cutaways are the currency

A cutaway is any shot that is not the main action: a face in the audience, hands on a keyboard, a program folded in a lap, feet on a stage floor, the conductor's back, an empty chair, a curtain edge, light through a window.

Cutaways solve every editing problem you will have. They cover a jump cut. They compress ten minutes into eight seconds. They give the eye a rest. They let you cheat time.

Shoot three times as many as you think you need. You will still run out. Get them during applause, during pauses, before the show, after the show, during anything that is not the moment you must not miss.

**One cutaway that is not a cutaway, and it is the most repeated mistake in music video work.** A tight shot of a specific note being fingered, a specific chord being struck, a specific bow stroke, is useless as filler. You cannot lay it over a different moment, because the viewer hears one note and watches a different one being played, and everybody notices. Shoot hands doing something unspecific: waiting, turning a page, resting on the keys, lifting away. Those cut anywhere. A recognizable note does not.

Two more, from concert directors. **Do not linger on the wrong performer**, holding on a face while somebody else is singing. And **do not cut too often.** Switching angles rapidly, and giving every shot the same length, reads as mechanical. Cut on the phrasing, not on the beat.

**Never cut inside a gesture.** A cut placed in the middle of a movement, or in the middle of a spoken beat, jars every time. Wait for the gesture to complete.

## 8. Handheld has a focal length limit

Braced properly, elbows in, two hands, breathing out, a wide lens handheld is fine. Past about 50mm, handheld footage shakes in a way that is visible and irreparable, and the longer the lens the worse it is exponentially.

Long lens footage needs the tripod, a monopod, a wall, a railing, a seat back, or a knee. Plan the long shots for wherever you can brace.

## 9. Audio is half the film, and it is fragile

Reverb destroys consonants. A hall that flatters a choir will make dialogue unintelligible. Music survives a bad room. Speech does not.

Camera microphones are usable for music in a good room, and for ambient texture (applause, laughter, a rustling audience) almost anywhere. They are rarely usable for dialogue from a house seat. If a spoken line has to land, you need to be close, or you need the venue's feed, or you need to plan the edit so that line is not required.

Applause and laughter are not noise. They are the sound of the event working, and they are frequently the best audio you will capture all night.

If you record audio on a separate device rather than into the camera, the two machines have different clocks, and they drift. Sync will look perfect at the start of a forty minute take and be visibly wrong by the end. Line the waveforms up at the moment you actually need, not at the head of the file, and expect to re line them up for each shot you use.

## 10. Picture profile

If you do not yet know how to color grade, do not shoot in a flat or log profile because a forum told you to. Flat footage that is never graded looks worse than a neutral profile straight out of camera. Shoot a standard or neutral profile, slightly under contrast, and grade lightly later. Add log to your life when you have edited five reels, not before.

## The five sentence summary

Hold every shot for at least six seconds and never reframe while rolling. Set shutter to double the frame rate and forget it exists. Lock white balance, lock ISO, do not let autofocus hunt. You cannot cut two similar shots together, so the locked off wide is the most valuable thing you own. Shoot far more cutaways than seem reasonable, because they will save every edit you make.
