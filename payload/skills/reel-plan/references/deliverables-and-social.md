# The deliverables, and what the platforms actually reward

The instinct is to cut one beautiful horizontal piece and crop a vertical out of it. The evidence says that is backwards, and the reason is worth understanding before it shapes a shot list.

A caveat on the evidence: most of what follows comes from social media marketing sources rather than platform published data, and the numbers vary between them (the proportion of viewers watching muted is cited anywhere from forty to eighty five percent). Treat the direction as reliable and the decimal places as not.

---

## Three products, not one

**1. The archival master.** Horizontal, two to three minutes, for the client's website and YouTube. Watched deliberately, with sound, by people who already care. This is the thing that feels like the deliverable, and it is the least contested part of the plan.

**2. The hero cut.** Vertical, **fifteen to thirty seconds.** This is the lead social asset and the one that actually travels. Reels under fifteen seconds get meaningfully more replays than reels over sixty, and completion rate, not length, is what the platform rewards. A twenty second piece finished by seventy percent of viewers beats a ninety second piece finished by twenty five percent.

**3. The story piece.** Vertical, sixty to ninety seconds, when there is a genuine arc worth that long. Expect lower completion. Make it because the story deserves it, not because it is the default.

The original plan said "a two to three minute master, and a sixty to ninety second vertical pulled from it." The master survives. The vertical does not: sixty to ninety seconds is the long, low retention end of the range, and pulling it from a finished horizontal cut is the worst of the available paths.

---

## The muted feed, and why it is brutal here

Reels and Shorts autoplay silent. Somewhere between half and the overwhelming majority of viewers never turn sound on.

For performing arts this is close to an existential problem, because **the art is the sound.** A choir reel with the audio muted is a video of people standing still with their mouths open.

The response is not to give up on sound. It is to build the vertical cut **silent first**:

- **Burned in text carries the meaning.** The piece, the composer or choreographer, the performer, the venue. Placed inside the safe area, away from the platform's own interface.
- **A hook line in the first frame**, as text. Something that creates a question. "Watch the front row." "This is one take." "Wait for the key change."
- **An explicit invitation to unmute**, placed just before the payoff. This is how creators whose work is fundamentally audio convert the silent majority at the exact moment it matters. It feels crude. It works.
- **Captions on any spoken word**, always. Comedy and theatre are unwatchable muted without them.

The horizontal master does not need any of this. It is watched with intent.

---

## Vertical: the crop problem, and what is actually true about it

A proscenium stage is a horizontal composition. It has been one since the seventeenth century. Cropping it to nine by sixteen throws away roughly forty four percent of the frame and was never composed to survive that.

You will read that this also destroys resolution. **For your gear, that part is false, and it matters.** Shoot 4K horizontal, which is 3840 by 2160. Crop a nine by sixteen slice from it and you get 1215 by 2160, which is *larger* than the 1080 by 1920 that a vertical deliverable needs. You lose no resolution at all.

What you lose is **composition**, and that is the real cost. So the fix is compositional, not technical:

- **Shoot 4K, and compose center safe** on any shot you intend to use vertically. Keep the subject that matters near the middle third, knowing a vertical crop is coming.
- **Shoot some frames for the crop deliberately.** A tight shot on a single performer survives a vertical crop beautifully. A full company on a wide stage never will.
- **Accept that some material is horizontal only.** A full orchestra, a corps de ballet, a company bow. Those belong to the archival master. For the vertical, feature one soloist, one section, one face. Do not try to squeeze ninety choristers into a phone screen.

The professional answer, shooting oversized and cropping both from one high resolution source, is exactly what a 4K camera already lets you do. You do not need a second vertical camera. You need to remember, while framing, that a vertical crop is coming.

---

## What actually performs, which is not the performance

The consistent finding, across arts marketing sources, is that polished full stage performance footage is not the highest engagement material. What outperforms it:

- **Rehearsal and process.** Raw, unpolished, close. This has become its own content genre. The Royal Ballet built a social presence on rehearsals, costume fittings, and dancer profiles rather than on performances.
- **Personality.** Performers as people, named, talking, warming up, waiting in the wings.
- **Reaction.** The audience changing: a skeptical room won over, a standing ovation, a face in tears, a child copying a gesture. There is no rigorous study proving reaction shots beat performance footage, so hold this loosely. But the pattern is well established that the drama lives in the *change* in the watcher, and reaction material is powerful as a **hook or a payoff inside** a performance cut rather than as a replacement for it.

There is a business conclusion buried in this, and it is the most valuable thing in this file. **Most arts organizations already have someone recording the show.** Almost none of them have anyone shooting the twenty minutes before it, or the faces in row three. The archival master is a commodity. The rehearsal footage, the backstage vertical, the reaction cutaways, and the short hero cut are not, and they are the assets the organization actually needs and cannot get.

If this ever becomes a paid package, that is the package. Not a better recording of the concert.

---

## Shot list consequences

Everything above collapses into four instructions that change how the event gets shot.

1. Shoot 4K, and frame center safe on anything destined for vertical.
2. Deliberately bank at least three tight shots on single subjects, composed to survive a vertical crop, one of which is the hero cut's payoff.
3. Bank reaction and cutaway material aggressively. It is the hook, the payoff, and the thing that rescues every edit.
4. Arrive early and shoot the room before it fills, the performers before they are performers, the empty stage lit. This is the material with the highest engagement and the lowest competition, and it costs you twenty minutes.
