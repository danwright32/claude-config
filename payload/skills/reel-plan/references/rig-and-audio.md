# Rig, audio, and rights

Three things to settle before writing a shot list. The third one can cancel the shoot, so read it first.

---

# Part 1. Rights and consent

**Ask these before the shoot. Never after. A reel that cannot be published is three weekends of your life.**

## Is the work original, or licensed?

This is the first question, and for musicals and plays it is close to the only question.

**A licensed musical or play** (from MTI, Concord, Samuel French, Dramatists Play Service, and the rest) is licensed under what the trade calls **grand rights**, and a standard performance license grants the right to stage the work and **explicitly does not grant the right to record it in any form.** MTI states plainly that its license does not include recording, that the authors retain the sole right to decide if their work is ever recorded, and that even an archival or classroom recording violates that right. Companies are required to print "the videotaping or other video or audio recording of this production is strictly prohibited" in the program.

That warning is aimed at the audience, but it describes the law that binds the company too. **If the production is a licensed work, assume you may not shoot video of it, and assume the company cannot give you permission it does not have.** Recording rights would have to be separately negotiated with the licensor by the producer, in advance. Ask to see it in writing.

**An original work,** where the writer and composer are the people who hired you, is the good case. The rights sit with the people in the room, and permission is a conversation rather than a negotiation with a rights house. Get it in writing anyway, naming: the reel, the client's use, and your use of it in your own portfolio and marketing.

## Music rights, when the music is not original

Two separate copyrights exist in any piece of recorded music: the **composition** and the **sound recording**. A performance of a public domain composition (Brahms, Bach, Handel) is safe on the composition side, and the recording you make is yours, so a choir singing Brahms is genuinely clear.

But: a choir singing a living composer's setting, or a dance company performing to a commercial track played over the house speakers, is not. Your camera captures the track, the platform's automatic content matching hears it, and the reel is muted or blocked, after you have edited it. If the house is playing recorded commercial music, treat the event's audio as unpostable and plan a silent cut with a licensed bed instead. That changes the shot list substantially, because you lose sync and need far more coverage and far more slow motion.

## Union performers

If the performers are under an Actors' Equity contract, recording and distribution are governed by that contract. Some Equity agreements have built in allowances for promotional footage; many do not, and anything outside the contract needs written permission from Equity's media department, obtained by the producer. Ask the producer: is this an Equity production, and does the contract or an approved media agreement permit promotional video. If the answer is vague, the answer is no.

## Children on camera

Filming children in the audience at a public performance is legal. **Using their faces to market your own photography business is a different act, and it is commercial use.** Commercial use of an identifiable minor's likeness requires a release signed by a parent or guardian. Delivering the reel to the client for the client's own use is one thing. Putting it on your website to win future bookings is another, and it is the thing this whole project is aimed at.

Practical answers, in order of preference:

- Ask the presenter whether their ticketing or entry terms already grant photography and video consent, and whether it extends to a hired photographer's promotional use. Many venues have this. Get the language.
- Shoot the audience so that children are **not identifiable**: from behind, over a shoulder, hands, feet, silhouettes against the stage, a wide of the whole room where no face reads, focus on the backs of heads with the stage sharp beyond them.
- Feature the performers, who are adults and under contract, in the version that goes on your site, and give the client the version with the children's faces in it.

## Venue rules

Carnegie Hall, Lincoln Center, and most major halls have published policies on recording, and a hired photographer's permissions come from the presenter, not from the venue's general public policy. Ask the presenter what they cleared with the venue, and whether it covers video as well as stills. Do not assume your stills credential extends to motion.

## The one paragraph to send before every shoot

Ask the client, in writing, for: confirmation the work is original or that recording rights exist, confirmation that any recorded music used in performance is cleared or absent, confirmation of Equity status and media permission if applicable, confirmation of the venue's video permission, and confirmation that you may use the finished reel in your own portfolio and marketing. If children will be identifiable, ask what consent the presenter collects at the door.

# Part 2. Rig modes

You have two bodies, two lenses, and a tripod. The venue decides how they are arranged, and the arrangement is inverse to your freedom.

## Pinned

Formal halls, assigned positions, Carnegie. You cannot move, so the tripod costs you nothing.

- **Body one, on the tripod: the safety wide.** Framed to hold the full stage with a little air, locked off, rolling continuously through entire numbers. Do not touch it. This is the most valuable footage you will shoot, because it is the angle you can always cut back to, and it is the only thing that lets you cut two views of the same musical moment.
- **Body two, in hand: stills, and tight video from the one position.** Long lens, braced on a knee or a seat back, because handheld past about 50mm shakes.

You get real cutting ability and one angle of view. The reel will feel composed and slightly distant. Cutaways must come from where you sit.

## Roaming

Churches, lobbies, black boxes, anywhere you can walk. Movement is worth more than the tripod, so both bodies come with you on two focal lengths.

- No safety wide. **This is the cost, and it is severe.** Without a continuous wide, you can never cut two angles of the same moment, so each passage appears exactly once, uncut, from wherever you were standing when it happened.
- Coverage comes from walking, and from banking a large number of cutaways during applause and pauses.
- Plan your positions in advance against the running order. Decide where you will be for the peak before the peak arrives, because you cannot be crossing the room when it lands.

Consider a hybrid: park a body on the tripod for the one number that matters most, roam for the rest. You lose the second lens for six minutes and gain the ability to cut the climax.

## Passes

**Default to one pass, forever.** Then ask, always, whether a second exists:

- **A dress rehearsal or photo call.** In professional theatre this is the norm, not a favor. Companies routinely hold a final dress designated for photographers, full costume, full tech, no audience, and photographers shoot it in preference to the performance. It is the single most valuable access you can ask for. It costs the company nothing, and most photographers new to theatre do not know to ask.
- **A repeated performance the same day.** Then pass one is coverage and reconnaissance, and pass two is where every ambitious shot goes, because by then you know the blocking, the laughs, and the light.
- **A soundcheck.** Free, unlit, unpeopled, but you can walk anywhere, test the shutter against the LED fixtures, find your positions, and shoot the room empty.

The rehearsal has no audience, which means it cannot give you the thing that is often the actual story. Shoot the rehearsal for the stage, and the performance for the room.

# Part 3. Audio tiers

Establish which tier you are in **before** planning shots, because the tier decides whether you are allowed to show a face making a sound.

## Tier A. A recording of this performance

Your camera's audio, your own recorder placed in the room, or the venue's board feed. **Only these can carry sync.** Only in this tier may you show a mouth singing, a bow drawing, a punchline landing.

If you place your own recorder: a small stereo recorder in front of the ensemble, above the heads of the back row and a few metres out, gets you far more than a camera microphone will. Err toward too close rather than too far, because you can add reverb later and you can never remove it. Remember that a separate recorder's clock drifts against the camera's, so align the waveforms at the moment you are using, not at the head of the file.

A board feed is often mono and mixed for the room rather than for a recording, which is not the same as mixed for you. Ask early, take it, and treat it as one ingredient rather than the answer.

## Tier B. Camera audio only

Fine for music in a decent room. Fine anywhere for texture: applause, laughter, a rustling audience, a hall going quiet. **Rarely usable for dialogue from a house seat**, because reverberation destroys consonants while leaving music intact. If a line must be heard, get close or cut the line.

## Tier C. A supplied studio recording of the same piece

Sounds like the best of all worlds and is a trap. It is a different take at a different tempo, so **it will not sync to your picture.** Usable only as a bed under material that is not sync critical: cutaways, faces in the audience, slow motion, hands from behind, an empty stage. Never over a visible mouth or bow.

## Tier D. A licensed library track

Same sync constraint as tier C, and no rights problem. Choose it before the shoot so you can plan the cut against its build. Instrumental. Cleared for commercial use. Matched to the event's dignity.

Most reels are a blend: a licensed bed carrying the montage, punctured by two or three pockets of tier A sync sound where a line, a laugh, or a final chord lands.
