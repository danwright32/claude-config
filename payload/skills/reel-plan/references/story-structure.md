# Story, not montage. Structure, not formula.

This is the most important file in the skill. Everything else is craft. This is the reason to bother.

Two ideas, and they must not be confused with each other.

**Story is mandatory.** Something changes, and the change is visible. Without it you have a montage.

**Structure is a choice.** How the change is revealed. There are many ways, and using the same one every time produces a body of work where every reel is the same reel with different music.

The old failure is a montage. The new failure, the one that arrives after you learn to tell a story, is a formula. Guard against both.

---

## Part 1. Story: what has to be true every time

First, a correction to how this is usually said. **A sizzle reel is a montage.** So is a film trailer. Montage is the form, not the failure, and anyone in the trade will tell you so.

**The failure is a montage with no arc.** Good moments, all weighted the same, in an order that could be shuffled without loss. No hook, no escalation, nothing at stake, and it stops rather than concludes. That is what most event highlight reels are.

**The fix is a change of state.** How things were, what happened, how things are now. Those are not three equal slices of running time. They are three conditions, and a reel can imply two of them while showing only one.

If you cannot name what changed, you have a shuffle. Cut it however you like.

There is a second, related law, from trailer editing: **energy must be modulated, not constant.** Hook hard, then drop back, then build. In Derek Lieu's formulation, if a trailer feels high energy all the time, then none of it will feel high energy. A reel that is loud from the first frame to the last has no peak, because a peak is defined by what precedes it.

### Where the change comes from

A live performance rarely hands you a plot. Nobody wants something, meets an obstacle, and overcomes it. So the change has to be found, and there are four places it lives.

**The room changes.** Empty and lit, then filling, then transformed by what happens in it. Reliable in every discipline. Requires only that you were there before anyone else.

**The audience changes.** Restless becomes still. Skeptical becomes held. A room of toddlers stops squirming. The strongest arc when available, because a change in the watcher is more legible than a change in the performer.

**The performer changes.** A person waiting in the wings, then a performer, then a person again at the bow. The curtain call is often the best footage of the night because it is the moment the artifice drops.

**The work changes.** The piece builds, resolves, releases. The weakest arc used alone, because the audience already experienced it and the reel becomes a summary. Use it as a layer under another arc, rarely as the whole.

The strong reels braid two together. The room fills while the performer waits. The music builds while a child's face opens.

### The shooting consequence that never changes

Whatever structure you choose, **the material for a beginning and an end mostly does not exist during the performance.** The empty stage under work lights. The doors opening. A performer stretching, checking a prop, breathing. The silence after the last note. The bow. The room emptying.

Shoot all of it, always, even when the structure you have chosen will not use it. It costs twenty minutes and it cannot be manufactured afterward. What you do with it is a structural decision made later. Whether you have it is decided on the day.

**Do not stop rolling when the music stops.** The end of the piece is not the end of your story.

## Part 2. Structure: pick one, and do not pick the same one twice

Each of these is a legitimate way to reveal a change. Pick the one that fits the event, not the one that worked last time.

**Trailer.** The borrowed film grammar, and the default for anything sold as a promo. Cold open on one high energy moment that needs no context. Drop back and introduce the world. Escalate, with the music and the cuts intensifying. Peak, and cut to the **title card** (show, dates, venue). One striking shot after the card as the button. Note where the title lands: at the end, not the front.

**Bookend.** Empty stage, the event, empty stage again. The same frame at the start and the end, meaning something different the second time. Reliable, clean, and the one that becomes formula fastest. Roughly fifteen, seventy, fifteen percent.

A warning about the bookend, because it collides with the hook. **A slow empty room opener is documentary grammar, not trailer grammar.** The first six seconds decide whether anyone stays. On a two or three minute master, watched deliberately, the quiet open is right. On a sixty second social piece it can lose the room before the story starts. Capture the empty room material always, then decide where it goes: often as a breath immediately after the cold open, rather than as frame one.

**In medias res.** Open at the peak, at full intensity, then drop back and show how it was arrived at. Steals from the middle to buy the opening. Excellent when the peak is extraordinary and the build is slow.

**The single moment.** No montage at all. One passage, forty seconds, barely cut, allowed to play. The change happens inside it. This requires a genuinely extraordinary moment and the nerve not to decorate it. It is the most confident thing you can do and it looks nothing like anyone else's reel.

**Portrait.** Follow one person through the event: one chorister, one dancer, the conductor, a child in the third row. The change is theirs. Everything else is context. The most intimate structure and the easiest to sell to a client, because it has a face.

**Reverse.** Open with the aftermath. An empty stage, a folded program on a seat, the last person leaving. Then show what caused it. The whole reel becomes an explanation.

**Parallel.** Two lines intercut that resolve into one. Stage and audience. Rehearsal and performance. A soloist's hands and the conductor's hands. Strong when the event has a genuine duality, hollow when it does not.

**Question and answer.** Pose something visually in the first seconds that the reel must resolve. A room of restless toddlers. An empty hall. A performer's face before an entrance. Then answer it.

**Accumulation.** A motif returns, changed each time. A gesture, a frame, a face, a chord. Musical form used as film form. Difficult, and when it works nobody else can copy it.

### Choosing

The structure should be argued for from the event, not selected from habit. A children's show with a restless room wants question and answer. A concert with one transcendent movement wants the single moment. A dance recital with a dozen equally good pieces wants portrait, because a montage of twelve pieces is the definition of the thing we are avoiding.

Write down, on the plan, **why this structure and not another.** If the answer is "it works," pick a different one.

## Part 3. Avoiding formula

Consistency of **voice** is your brand. Consistency of **structure** is a rut.

Voice is how it looks: the restraint, the intimacy, the willingness to hold a shot, the way you treat a face. That should be recognizable across every reel, and it is what a client is buying when they hire you rather than someone else.

Structure is the order in which things are revealed. It should be different nearly every time.

**The rule this skill enforces:** before writing a plan, read the previous plans in the same directory. Do not reuse the previous plan's structure. Do not reuse the previous plan's opening device. If the last three reels all opened on an empty stage, the next one may not, whatever its structure.

Name the previous structures on the plan, so the choice is visible and deliberate.

Two other things that make reels look identical, independent of structure:

- **The same shot sizes in the same order.** Wide, medium, tight, repeat. Vary which size opens a sequence.
- **The same music shape.** A soft build into a swell, every time. If you choose the bed, choose differently.

---

## Part 4. Wide, medium, tight

The positive statement of the jump cut rule, which has a formal name: **the 30 degree rule.** Two successive shots of the same subject must differ by roughly thirty degrees of angle, or by a full step of shot size. Less than that and the image jumps and the viewer sees the edit.

So you must **carry** all three sizes of any subject you intend to cut on. That is a physical constraint of editing, not an aesthetic preference.

**But carrying three sizes is not a licence to cut three times.** Over cutting is a specific, named error in concert direction: switching angles too often, and giving every clip the same length, reads as mechanical and monotonous. Orchestra directors deliberately pace their cuts to musical phrasing, and return to the conductor between angles rather than ping ponging.

The rule, stated correctly: **carry a wide, a medium, and a tight of every subject so that any cut you make is legal. Then cut on the phrasing, not on the beat, and cut less often than you want to.**

- **Wide.** Where are we. The whole stage, the whole room, the whole body. In dance, non negotiable, and it must include feet.
- **Medium.** Who is doing what. A performer from the waist, a section, two people in relationship.
- **Tight.** What it feels like. Hands, a face, an instrument, an eye. Where the emotion is, and the shot beginners forget because it feels intrusive.

Three rules of use:

1. **A sequence needs all three.** Any order, but all three present.
2. **Never cut from a size to itself.** Wide to a slightly different wide reads as a mistake.
3. **You will be short of tights.** Everyone comes home with too many wides. Over collect tights deliberately.

In **pinned** mode the locked tripod holds the wide continuously while the handheld body collects mediums and tights. This is why pinned mode cuts so well.

In **roaming** mode there is no continuous wide, so all three sizes must be collected across time and across the room, and you must decide in advance which beat gets which size. Write it down. You will not improvise it correctly while walking.

## The test, before the shoot

Answer these on the plan, in writing.

- **What changes?** One sentence.
- **Which structure**, and why this one rather than another?
- **What were the last three reels' structures?** This one is not among them.
- **What is the first shot,** and what does it make the viewer want to know?
- **What is the last shot,** and what does it leave behind?
- **Which beat carries the turn?**
- **Does every beat have a wide, a medium, and a tight?**

If the structure question cannot be answered from the event, the plan is running on habit.
